How does this model work?

Every topic starts with one point. Watching a video adds one point to its topic. A topic’s chance is its points divided by the total. Real feeds use many more signals. This experiment runs in your browser and resets when you reload.

Our research · 21 students · Astana IT University

We know, but we don’t control.

We asked 21 students about their social media habits, privacy and control. Most of them are online every day, and most of them know how feeds work. But knowing did not give them control.

67%use social media several times a day
81%know that platforms collect data about what they watch and like
76%say they understand why videos are recommended to them (4–5 of 5)
62%did not change privacy settings in six months, or don’t remember
Main finding

Knowledge does not become freedom.

Three out of four students understand recommendations. Only one out of four feels in control of what they end up watching.

Understand the feed76%
Feel in control24%
Feel low control38%

Method: Google Forms survey without names, 9 questions, November 2026. Scale answers: 4–5 counted as high, 1–2 as low. Limitation: the sample is small and 71% are third-year students, so the results show a trend, not a rule for all students.

Philosophical question

Who shapes the options?

If you can skip any video, how free is your choice when a platform decides which videos you see first?


John Stuart Mill · Individual liberty

Mill valued the freedom to try different ways of living. A feed can introduce you to a new interest. If it keeps repeating familiar topics, what opportunities do you have to discover a different point of view?

Immanuel Kant · Autonomy

Kant connects autonomy with acting on principles you can rationally endorse. Applied to a feed, ask yourself whether continuing to scroll serves a purpose you chose. A recommendation can support that purpose or distract you from it.

Lawrence Lessig · Code is law

Lessig argued that in digital space, code works like law. The design of an app decides what we can and cannot do. Nobody forbids us to search for new topics, but the feed is built so that we rarely need to.

Eli Pariser · The filter bubble

Pariser called algorithms “prediction engines” that build a unique world of information for each person. We feel free because the feed looks endless, but it keeps showing us what we already like. This is soft paternalism: we are guided while keeping the feeling of choice.

Three ethical questions

When does a suggestion become manipulation?

Influence becomes ethically troubling when a platform exploits a habit to keep someone watching against their considered wishes. A useful test is whether the person would still endorse the suggestion if they understood its purpose.

What do platforms owe users about their data?

People need a clear explanation of which activities shape their recommendations and a meaningful way to limit that use. A long policy alone gives a student little help deciding what to share.

Who is responsible for a narrow feed?

Users can seek unfamiliar sources. Platforms also choose what to rank and which controls to offer. More responsibility falls on those who can change the system for everyone.

Ethical reflection

The ethics of our own project.

A project about manipulation should not manipulate. We asked the same questions about our own work.

1. What are the risks?

  • The survey collects personal answers about habits.
  • The sample is small, so results could be overgeneralized.
  • The guide could sound like moralizing and make people feel guilty.
  • The feed lab is a simplified model, not a real algorithm.

2. Who might be harmed?

  • Respondents, if their answers could be linked to them.
  • Students who already feel anxious about screen time.
  • Readers who trust our numbers more than they should.

3. How do we reduce the risks?

  • The survey does not ask for names or student IDs.
  • We state the limits of our sample openly.
  • The guide gives suggestions, not rules: the choice stays yours.
  • The feed lab explains its model and stores nothing.
Practical guide

Choose your next scroll.

  1. Set an intention. Before opening the app, name one thing you want to watch or learn.
  2. Change the signal. Use “Not interested” where available, or search for a topic your feed rarely shows.
  3. Review one setting. Check watch history or personalization controls and choose what fits your preferences.
  4. Compare the result. Look at your next five recommendations. How many fit your original intention?
Measured impact · 15 students · before/after experiment

More control, but not full control.

We tested our guide. Students answered questions, used the three steps in their own TikTok, Instagram or YouTube feed for about five minutes, and answered again.

2.9 → 3.9average feeling of control (out of 5)
27% → 67%students who feel high control (4–5 of 5)
2.1 → 3.4of the next 5 videos matched the student’s own goal
73%found a feed setting they did not know about
What students said

“I felt more in control, but the app still decides most of my feed.”

Another student wrote: “Even if you press ‘not interested’, the feed can still send you videos you were never interested in.”

Conclusion: small actions gave students more control, but the algorithm still sets the limits. Nobody answered that they would not use the steps again (47% yes, 53% maybe). Limitations: 15 participants, before and after were measured on the same day, and participants knew the goal of the experiment, so this shows a short-term effect only.
